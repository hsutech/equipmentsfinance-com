The best equipment financing company in the USA depends on the borrower and the asset. For many owners, the strongest first move is to compare a marketplace, an equipment-focused lender, a bank or SBA lender, and a specialist for the industry or credit profile. The goal is not to find the loudest advertised rate. The goal is to find a structure that fits the equipment, the business cash flow, the seller timeline and the paperwork you can support.

Search results for this topic are mostly BOFU comparison pages. They rank providers, show tables, explain qualification factors and answer practical questions about equipment loans, SBA options, collateral, repayment terms and credit requirements. That is the right format here too, but with one important caution: no ranking can promise approval, pricing or the best outcome for every business. Equipment financing is still underwriting. Lenders look at the borrower, the equipment, the seller and the payment fit.

Plain-English recommendation: compare at least three realistic paths before you apply: a marketplace for breadth, a direct equipment lender for asset-specific speed, and a bank or SBA option if you have stronger financials and can wait longer.

How we compare equipment financing companies

This comparison uses public lender positioning, current SERP patterns and finance fundamentals rather than invented customer reviews or first-hand testing. The current organic results favor tables and provider shortlists, but the strongest pages also explain how the decision should change for newer businesses, used equipment, large-ticket purchases, lower credit scores and borrowers who want SBA-backed financing.

The main comparison criteria are:

  • Borrower fit: time in business, credit profile, revenue and documentation depth.
  • Equipment fit: new versus used, titled vehicles, heavy machinery, medical equipment, restaurant equipment, office equipment or mixed packages.
  • Speed: whether the provider is built for fast quotes or a slower bank/SBA review.
  • Transparency: how clearly the provider explains product type, possible fees, repayment cadence and required documents.
  • Total cost risk: not just the payment, but term length, down payment, fees, prepayment terms and whether the structure fits the asset's useful life.

For government-backed alternatives, the SBA 7(a) program can support machinery and equipment purchases, while the SBA 504 program is designed for major fixed assets through Certified Development Companies. Tax treatment is separate from financing; the IRS explains depreciation basics in Publication 946. For a broad current market comparison, Buy Side from The Wall Street Journal maintains a page on business equipment loans.

1. Lendio: broad comparison marketplace

Lendio is a strong starting point when you do not know which lender category fits your deal. A marketplace can help compare equipment financing, term loans, lines of credit and SBA options from one application path. That breadth matters when the equipment is urgent but the borrower profile is not obvious: newer business, uneven credit, mixed assets or uncertain down payment.

The tradeoff is that a marketplace is not the same as a final lender decision. The useful work happens after the match, when you compare the real offer. Look for the lender name, repayment frequency, origination fees, prepayment language, collateral requirements and whether the quote is an equipment loan, lease or working-capital product being used for equipment.

2. Crest Capital: equipment-focused direct lending

Crest Capital is worth comparing when you already know the equipment, seller and timing. Equipment-focused lenders tend to understand asset details better than general-purpose lenders. That can help when the purchase involves machinery, titled vehicles, software bundled with equipment, installation or used equipment bought from a dealer or private seller.

For this path, the borrower should be ready with a quote or invoice, year, make, model, serial number, seller contact and delivery timing. If the equipment is used, photos, condition notes and comparable listings can make the conversation cleaner. Owners comparing new and used assets should also review our guide to new vs. used equipment financing.

3. Balboa Capital: direct small-business equipment financing

Balboa Capital is another direct lender commonly considered in equipment financing searches. It can be a useful comparison point for business owners who want a direct online process rather than starting with a bank branch. It is especially relevant when the owner wants to know quickly whether the purchase is likely financeable.

The key is to clarify product type. Some providers use equipment loan, equipment lease and equipment finance language in ways that can sound similar but behave differently. Ask who owns the equipment during the term, whether there is a purchase option, how taxes and insurance are handled, whether there is a blanket lien, and what happens if you want to pay off early.

4. National Funding: speed-oriented business financing

National Funding often appears in searches where speed, easier qualification or alternative business lending matter. That can be useful for replacement equipment, restaurant failures, delivery vehicles, jobsite equipment or a purchase that cannot wait through a long underwriting cycle.

The caution is cost. Fast financing can be practical, but daily or weekly repayment, factor pricing or shorter terms may feel heavier than a conventional monthly equipment loan. Before choosing speed, owners should compare the payment against payroll, rent, insurance, fuel, inventory and slow-season cash flow. A fast approval that strains operations is not a good outcome.

5. Bank of America or Wells Fargo: traditional bank path

Traditional banks can be attractive for established businesses with cleaner financials, stronger credit and existing banking relationships. They may be better suited for borrowers who can provide tax returns, financial statements, bank records and a clear business case for the equipment. Banks may also offer SBA products or conventional secured business loans depending on the borrower and asset.

The bank path is less ideal when the purchase is extremely urgent, the business is very new, the equipment is older, or the seller paperwork is thin. A bank may still be worth checking, but owners should not rely on a bank-only plan if the seller needs a quick answer. A second option can prevent the purchase from stalling.

6. SBA lender or CDC: documented, long-term option

SBA-backed financing can be a strong fit for qualified borrowers buying meaningful equipment, especially when the company can support a deeper review and does not need same-day funding. The 7(a) program can be used for machinery and equipment, and 504 loans can support major fixed assets that promote business growth and job creation.

This is not the fastest route. The SBA does not make most of these loans directly to the borrower; participating lenders and Certified Development Companies handle the process. Expect more documentation, more review and a longer timeline. The benefit is that the structure may be more attractive for borrowers who qualify and can wait.

7. Accion Opportunity Fund: mission-focused small-business lending

Accion Opportunity Fund is a useful comparison for smaller businesses, underserved entrepreneurs and owners who may not look perfect on a traditional bank checklist. It is not the right fit for every equipment purchase, and product availability can depend on the borrower, use case and location. Still, it belongs in the shortlist because financing access is not only about who has the largest advertised loan amount.

Owners should confirm whether the equipment category, state, business age and loan size fit before spending time on a full application. If the deal is larger or more specialized, compare Accion with a marketplace, a direct equipment lender and an SBA lender.

How to choose the right equipment financing company

Start with the equipment, not the lender. A $35,000 oven package, a $90,000 box truck, a $160,000 excavator and a $400,000 medical buildout do not create the same financing conversation. The asset type affects collateral value, useful life, insurance, seller documents, delivery timing and repair risk. That is why a provider that works well for one purchase may be a poor fit for another.

Then check the borrower profile. If the business is established, profitable and organized, a bank or SBA path may be worth the extra time. If the owner needs broad comparison, a marketplace can surface options quickly. If the asset details are complicated, an equipment-focused lender may understand the deal faster. If credit is imperfect, focus on providers willing to look at cash flow, collateral and business context, but read the cost carefully.

Finally, compare offers side by side. Do not compare only monthly payment. Compare amount financed, down payment, term, fees, estimated total repayment, repayment frequency, collateral, personal guarantee, insurance requirements, prepayment terms and documentation conditions. If two offers use different pricing language, ask for an APR-equivalent explanation or a total-dollar repayment estimate.

Questions to ask before applying

  • Is this a loan, lease, equipment finance agreement or working-capital product?
  • Who owns the equipment during and after the term?
  • What is the total amount paid if every payment is made as scheduled?
  • Are payments monthly, weekly or daily?
  • Is there an origination fee, documentation fee, prepayment penalty or payoff discount?
  • Does the lender require a personal guarantee, blanket lien or only the equipment as collateral?
  • What documents are required before approval and before funding?
  • Can the lender finance used equipment, private-party sales, installation, delivery or soft costs?

Those questions keep the conversation grounded. A lender that answers clearly is easier to compare. A lender that avoids basic cost, term or ownership questions deserves more scrutiny.

FAQ

What is the best equipment financing company in the USA?

There is no single best company for every borrower. A marketplace may work for fast comparison, a bank may work for established borrowers, an SBA lender may work for lower-cost long-term financing, and an equipment-focused lender may work when the asset details matter most.

Is equipment financing easier to qualify for than an unsecured business loan?

It can be easier in some cases because the equipment often serves as collateral. That does not remove underwriting. Lenders still review credit, cash flow, time in business, equipment value, seller details and ability to repay.

Should I choose the lowest monthly payment?

Not automatically. A lower payment can help cash flow, but it may also mean a longer term, more total interest or a structure that does not match the equipment's useful life. Compare total cost and payment timing before choosing.

Can used equipment be financed?

Yes, used equipment can often be financed, but documentation becomes more important. Expect to provide year, make, model, serial number, hours or mileage, condition, seller information and proof that the value is reasonable.

How many lenders should I compare?

Three to five serious options is usually enough for a practical comparison. Include different lender types when possible: a marketplace, a direct equipment lender, a bank or SBA lender, and a specialist if your industry or credit profile calls for one.

What should I prepare before I apply?

Prepare the quote or invoice, seller contact, equipment description, expected delivery date, requested amount, down payment range, business details and a short explanation of how the equipment supports revenue, capacity, replacement or efficiency. A cleaner first package usually creates a better financing conversation.
