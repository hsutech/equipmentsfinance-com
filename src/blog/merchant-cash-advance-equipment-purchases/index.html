Why an MCA Can Be a Poor Fit for Equipment Purchases

Match equipment installation and useful life with payment timing before using a merchant cash advance.

By Aitor Tilla · October 17, 2026

In this article

  2. Count the whole installed cost
  3. Work through an installation-delay example
  4. Compare useful life with the recovery period
  5. Examine asset-specific alternatives
  6. Do not confuse revenue-linked language with equipment protection
  7. Treat a seller discount as one input

Equipment earns its return over time. A merchant cash advance can begin collecting money before the machine is delivered, installed or producing saleable work. That mismatch is the central reason to be cautious about using an MCA for a durable asset, even when funding arrives quickly and the equipment looks profitable over its useful life.

This article is educational information for US businesses, not individual financial, legal or tax advice. Examples are hypothetical. It does not identify a universally best financing product or promise approval, savings or a particular return from equipment.

Draw the equipment timeline before the funding timeline

List the dates for deposit, delivery, installation, inspection, training and first production. Then add the date the first customer payment is expected to reach the bank. The machine's arrival and its first cash contribution are not necessarily the same event.

Include dependencies outside the seller's control. Electrical work, building access, permits, compatible tooling or a specialist technician can affect readiness. Ask which dates are confirmed and which are estimates. A forecast based on the earliest possible installation can understate the cash needed during the transition.

Finally, place the proposed financing collections on that same timeline. If withdrawals begin immediately, the existing business must carry them until the asset contributes cash. Show that temporary burden explicitly rather than assuming the new equipment pays for itself from day one.

Our equipment loan versus lease guide helps separate ownership and use questions. The first decision here is more basic: whether the collection schedule fits the asset's operating cycle at all.

Count the whole installed cost

The invoice price may be only part of the project. Freight, installation, training, accessories, site preparation, insurance and initial consumables can require additional cash. Obtain written estimates where possible and avoid assigning zero to an unresolved cost merely because it is absent from the seller's quote.

If the advance covers only the machine invoice, the business may still need its reserve to make the asset usable. That reserve also supports ordinary operations and early collections. Counting it twice creates a false impression of affordability.

Distinguish financed costs from cash costs. A financing provider may accept some categories and exclude others. The seller may require a deposit before the provider releases funds. Record the actual sequence rather than treating “approved amount” as cash available on any date.

A modest contingency is sensible only when grounded in the project. Identify what it covers and what would happen if that amount were exceeded. A generic percentage cannot replace investigating a known installation uncertainty.

Work through an installation-delay example

Assume an invented $50,000 equipment purchase funded by an arrangement requiring $65,000 over 130 collection days, with equal $500 debits and no additional fees for simplicity. These are illustrative numbers, not available terms or an APR calculation.

If installation takes twenty collection days, $10,000 has been collected before the machine produces revenue. The funding may have paid the supplier, but the existing operation must supply that $10,000 while also paying normal bills. A ten-day delay adds another $5,000 of collections before productive use.

Now assume the installed machine is expected to add $6,000 a month in contribution after direct production costs but before financing and overhead. That expectation should be tested against actual orders, utilization and customer payment timing. It cannot be compared casually with a daily debit without a dated cash model.

The illustration explains the risk without predicting the outcome. A business with strong reserves could absorb a transition that another cannot. The relevant question is whether the whole business can carry the collections under a credible delay, not whether the asset has a positive lifetime return.

Compare useful life with the recovery period

A machine used for several years can create value long after a short funding arrangement ends. However, a long useful life does not make rapid collections affordable. The business needs enough cash during the collection period, not just eventual economic benefit.

Estimate the asset's contribution using realistic utilization. Deduct labor, materials, maintenance, utilities and other incremental costs. If the purchase replaces an existing machine, distinguish new revenue from savings or reduced downtime. Do not count all existing customer revenue as a benefit created by the replacement.

For example, avoiding a documented rental cost is different from assuming a new production line will immediately run at full capacity. Show each source of benefit separately and identify the evidence behind it. Purchase orders and service records are stronger inputs than an optimistic sales pitch.

Ask what happens if the equipment is unavailable for repair. Collections may continue even when production stops, depending on the agreement. Insurance, warranty coverage and maintenance arrangements have their own limits; none should be assumed to cover every lost receipt.

Examine asset-specific alternatives

An equipment loan or lease may offer a payment structure better aligned with use, but that is a hypothesis to test against written terms. Compare the deposit, payment dates, total cost, ownership position, end-of-term obligations and restrictions on use or disposal.

The SBA 7(a) overview includes purchasing and installing machinery among eligible uses. Qualification and lender review still apply. Do not assume an SBA-backed loan will close by the seller's deadline or cover every project cost.

Rental can reduce the immediate commitment when demand is uncertain, though it may cost more over a long period. A used machine may lower the purchase price while raising maintenance uncertainty. A staged purchase can preserve cash but delay capacity. Compare these tradeoffs in the same operating forecast.

Use our new versus used equipment financing discussion to structure that comparison. The goal is to match the purchase and funding to documented demand, rather than selecting a funding product first and then stretching the project to fit it.

Do not confuse revenue-linked language with equipment protection

A sales-linked arrangement may sound forgiving when production slows. Read how collections actually respond. Some products use scheduled amounts with an adjustment process; the contract determines the requirements and timing. Equipment failure does not itself explain how that process works.

The FTC's MCA discussion within its financing perspective identifies concerns about repayment pressure. It is a historical regulatory source, not a current price survey or a judgment about every provider.

Ask the provider to illustrate the agreement during a month with reduced receipts. Have a qualified adviser review guarantees, security interests and default provisions. If equipment is pledged elsewhere or subject to another agreement, do not assume additional financing creates no conflict.

Also check whether the arrangement limits changes to bank accounts, payment processors or other financing. Those provisions can affect operational decisions that otherwise seem unrelated to the equipment purchase.

Treat a seller discount as one input

A discount for paying the supplier quickly can make fast funding attractive. Compare the confirmed discount with all financing costs and the cash burden. A $2,000 discount does not justify an unknown financing charge or a schedule that prevents the business from operating the machine.

Ask whether the supplier can hold the price, stage payments or offer an alternative delivery arrangement. Any concession should be documented. Do not assume a verbal promise will survive a delay in financing approval.

Check what happens if delivery fails or the purchase is canceled after funding. The equipment contract and funding agreement may handle that event differently. Obtain advice on material obligations rather than assuming one cancellation automatically unwinds the other.

Use community stories as prompts, not purchase evidence

The merchant cash advance experiences discussed here are unverified anecdotes. They can suggest useful questions about collection pressure but cannot establish how a particular machine or contract will perform.

A business with immediate high-margin sales may describe a different outcome from one waiting for installation and customer acceptance. Without that context, copying another owner's decision is unreliable. Ask which assumptions in their story would actually hold in your operation.

Write a purchase-and-funding decision together

Before committing, record the installed cost, conservative readiness date, first expected receipt, contribution forecast and lowest projected cash balance. Add a delayed-installation case and a lower-utilization case. Confirm that essential operations remain funded in each plausible scenario.

If the purchase only works when everything happens on the earliest date and at full utilization, reduce the commitment or reconsider the funding. A durable asset can be worth buying while a particular MCA is still the wrong way to pay for it. Evaluate those two decisions together, with the operating timeline visible.
