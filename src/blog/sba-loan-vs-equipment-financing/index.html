Choose conventional equipment financing when a specific asset is the main purchase and the written offer fits your cash flow and delivery deadline. Consider SBA 7(a) when eligible equipment costs belong to a broader funding need, and SBA 504 when qualifying long-lived machinery is part of a major fixed-asset project. An SBA-backed loan can itself finance equipment: the useful comparison is between actual financing structures, not mutually exclusive labels.

For established businesses in Texas and across the United States, the decision often comes down to the amount of cash required at closing, the repayment schedule, collateral and certainty of funding. A lower advertised rate does not settle those questions. Use it to evaluate written offers for a planned equipment purchase or replacement.

When each option deserves a quote

Conventional equipment loan: a focused purchase

This route deserves a quote when you know the machine, seller and delivery schedule, and can fund any excluded expenses separately. A straightforward replacement may not need the same structure as a property purchase or acquisition. The lender still needs to assess repayment capacity and the asset; equipment value does not make approval automatic.

Ask whether freight, installation, taxes and software are financed or payable from cash. A quoted percentage of the equipment invoice may leave a larger cash requirement than expected. Also ask whether the offer is directly from a lender or arranged through a broker, and who will service the account.

SBA 7(a): a broader eligible funding requirement

The SBA 7(a) program permits machinery purchases and installation, working capital and other eligible uses, including multipurpose loans. Applications go through lenders. The SBA guarantee supports the lender; it does not relieve the borrower of repayment. The published maximum is $5 million, but that ceiling is not an indication of an individual business’s borrowing capacity.

Request a 7(a) assessment when the financing need extends beyond the machine. Ask the lender to identify which expenses qualify and whether the business meets program requirements, including whether the desired credit is available on reasonable terms without government support, and ability to repay. A cheaper-looking comparison quote alone does not establish SBA eligibility.

SBA 504: qualifying durable machinery

The SBA 504 program supports major fixed assets through Certified Development Companies working with senior lenders. Its listed equipment uses require a minimum remaining useful life of ten years. Working capital and inventory are excluded. An asset’s expected remaining life therefore matters more than its purchase price alone.

Ask a CDC to assess the proposed equipment and explain the complete financing package. Obtain the borrower contribution, separate payment schedules, closing expenses and any interim financing requirements. Do not treat the advertised rate on one component as the cost of the entire project.

Compare cash, payments and exit cost on the same basis

Send each provider the same equipment specification and itemized seller quote. Otherwise, the cheaper proposal may simply finance fewer costs. Record three separate figures: cash needed before delivery, scheduled payments during ownership and the amount due if you exit early. Keep operating reserves outside the down-payment calculation.

The SBA’s current lender guidance on 7(a) terms says rates may be fixed or variable and are negotiated within applicable maximums. It also describes fees and maturity rules. An allowed maximum rate is not a personalized quote. Ask for the actual rate basis, adjustment frequency and all borrower-paid fees rather than estimating your offer from a search-result range.

A lower monthly payment can still cost more

Consider two invented, fully amortizing loans of $100,000, with monthly payments and no fees. At a fixed 10% annual interest rate over five years, the payment is approximately $2,125 and total payments are approximately $127,482. At a fixed 9% over ten years, the payment is approximately $1,267 and total payments are approximately $152,011. These are arithmetic illustrations, not typical SBA or equipment-loan rates.

The longer loan releases roughly $858 of monthly cash but adds roughly $24,529 in total payments. Neither is automatically the right choice. Compare the machine’s expected productive life, the cash cushion needed for slow months and the likelihood of paying off early. Fees, variable rates and different funding amounts would change the calculation.

For an early replacement, request a written payoff estimate at a specified month. A principal balance, a contractual buyout and a total of remaining payments are different figures. Ask which applies. Our guide to equipment financing rates and costs explains additional quote terminology.

Funding speed: measure the deadline that matters

A supplier needs payment, not a prequalification email. Ask each provider to distinguish initial screening, credit approval, signed documents, satisfaction of conditions and cleared funds. A published approval turnaround may describe only one stage. It should not be used as a guaranteed equipment delivery date.

List unresolved conditions: seller verification, insurance, lien releases, asset inspection or installation acceptance. Ask who controls each step and whether funds go directly to the supplier. If a deposit is due first, confirm whether it is refundable and whether financing must close before you become bound to purchase.

For a time-sensitive replacement, compare the cost of a temporary rental or repaired existing machine with the cost of an available financing offer. Use expected lost contribution after avoidable expenses, not gross sales, when estimating downtime. That analysis is specific to your operation; no universal “SBA is too slow” rule can replace it.

Collateral, guarantees and existing debt

Do not assume an equipment loan exposes only the machine, or that every SBA transaction has identical security requirements. Ask for the exact collateral description, personal guarantee terms and any restrictions on additional borrowing or asset sales. Have an appropriate adviser explain obligations you do not understand before signing.

An established business may already have a lender with a lien affecting the proposed collateral. Share the existing debt schedule early and ask whether a release, consent or subordination is needed. A new approval does not resolve competing claims over an asset. Confirm how any required coordination affects the closing date.

Be equally careful with “finance now, refinance with SBA later.” Future eligibility, lender approval, transaction costs and payoff terms remain uncertain. Evaluate the first loan as an obligation you can afford to keep. Do not make affordability depend on an unapproved refinance.

A practical shortlist for three buying situations

Replacing a machine in an established Texas workshop

Request a conventional equipment quote for the specific replacement and a realistic supplier payment date. If there is time to compare an SBA-backed option, use identical purchase costs and an identical expected ownership period. The decision should account for cash reserves, downtime and early-payoff terms, not simply the lowest opening payment.

Adding equipment alongside an expansion

Separate the machine, approved installation work and operating needs. A 7(a) proposal may be worth assessing when the financing request has several eligible purposes. Compare it with a conventional equipment loan plus a clearly identified source for the remaining costs. Include all payments from both facilities when testing affordability.

Purchasing long-lived production machinery

Include a CDC assessment if the asset could meet 504 criteria. Compare the combined package with conventional financing and any relevant 7(a) proposal. Confirm remaining useful life, borrower contribution and responsibility for each closing condition. A long equipment life does not automatically mean that the longest available loan is desirable.

Businesses still preparing their first operating project can use our separate guide to SBA loans for startup equipment. This comparison instead assumes you are ready to request and evaluate specific financing proposals.

Request a decision-ready offer

Ask each shortlisted provider to put the following in writing:

  • The financed amount, excluded costs and total cash required before funding.
  • The payment amount, frequency, first due date and fixed or variable rate basis.
  • All fees, collateral, guarantees and financial reporting obligations.
  • The conditions still required to close and the expected supplier payment date.
  • The payoff method and restrictions on selling or replacing the equipment.

The FTC’s small-business financing guidance recommends checking who is behind an offer before sharing confidential information. Search placement is not evidence of trustworthiness. Establish whether you are dealing with a lender or an intermediary and how your application information will be used.

Frequently asked questions

Is an SBA loan a type of equipment financing?

It can be. An eligible SBA-backed loan can finance equipment. In this comparison, conventional equipment financing means a purchase loan without an SBA guarantee.

Is SBA financing always cheaper?

No. Compare actual rates, fees, payment schedules and payoff terms. A longer repayment period can lower monthly payments while increasing total interest paid.

Can an SBA 504 loan cover inventory or payroll?

No. SBA lists working capital and inventory as excluded uses for 504 financing. Discuss separate operating needs with the CDC and lender before committing to the project.

Does fast approval mean the seller will be paid immediately?

No. Approval can still be conditional on documents, insurance, inspections or other closing requirements. Ask for the expected disbursement date and outstanding conditions.

Should I plan to refinance an equipment loan with SBA later?

Only treat refinancing as a possibility, not a certainty. The later transaction requires eligibility, approval and a cost comparison. The original loan must remain affordable without it.
